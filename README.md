# Image Translator

A Chrome extension that translates the text inside images, in place.

- **Text in a bubble** (speech bubbles, caption boxes, white meme bars) is painted over in the bubble's own colour and relettered. The extension flood-fills the bubble to find the largest rectangle inside it, so a tall, narrow column of vertical Japanese becomes comfortably sized horizontal English.
- **Text on artwork** (sound effects, narration over a drawing, outlined lettering) is erased: exactly the letters and their outline, nothing around them. What was behind them is rebuilt from the artwork next to them, so screentone, hatching, grain and colour carry on through where the letters were. The translation is drawn on top in the lettering's own colours.
- **Every translation lands on its own text.** The extension finds all the lettering on the page itself, from the pixels — dialogue, narration, sound effects; Japanese, Korean, Chinese or Latin; dark or light, outlined or not, tilted, tiny or huge — and matches each of Gemini's translations to the lettering it describes, however far off Gemini's box is. When all of Gemini's boxes for an image are off the same way (shifted, or scaled as if the image were another size), that is noticed and taken out. Translations are set no larger than the original lettering, and words are never hyphenated just to fit.
- **Overlays are glued to the image.** The browser itself keeps each overlay on its image in every frame (CSS anchor positioning): while you scroll, in whatever scrolls (the page, a reader pane, a page whose `<body>` is the scroller), and while the image moves or resizes. Nothing trails behind and catches up.
- **Pages are ready before you get to them.** Images up to three screens ahead are translated in advance, also on readers that lazy-load their pages, and a request Gemini stalls on is sent again rather than waited out.
- **Reloads re-scan automatically.** The content script is registered for each site you enable, so every page load (refresh, back/forward, new visit) translates again. Results are cached, so a reload repaints within a fraction of a second, with no API call.

One Gemini request per image does OCR, translation and box detection together. Everything else (fetching, splitting, bubble detection, caching) happens locally.

## Install

1. Get a Gemini API key at <https://aistudio.google.com/apikey>.
2. Open `chrome://extensions`, turn on **Developer mode**, click **Load unpacked**, and pick this folder.
3. The settings page opens. Paste the key and click **Save key**. The extension checks it right away with one tiny real request to the selected model, the only check that proves translation will work.
4. Under **Google plan**, pick **Free tier** or **Billing enabled**. This fills in sensible request limits.
5. Go to a site with images, click the toolbar button, and switch on **Translate images on …**.

The extension has access to websites from the moment it's installed: it needs it to download images from the servers a site uses, which are rarely the site itself. It only ever reads pages on the sites you switch on.

**On a phone:** Android browsers that run Chrome extensions (Lemur, for example) open the extension's popup from their menu, sometimes as a page of its own; it then works on the web page you came from. There is no Alt key to peek at the original; use **Show original** in the popup. If the popup asks for access and nothing happens when you tap **Grant access**, the browser can't show that prompt: remove the extension and install it again, which grants it at install (paste your API key again afterwards).

Pick the target language in the popup, and the **Text size** of the translations (50–250%): open pages are set again at once, from what's already drawn, without asking Gemini again. Larger text takes only space nobody else uses: it grows into the free space around it, never over another translation, and inside a bubble it stays inside the bubble. Hold **Alt** to peek at the original; **Show original** in the popup toggles it for the tab.

**Updating:** unzip the new version over the old folder, then click the reload icon on the extension's card in `chrome://extensions`. Pages you translated before are redrawn from the saved answers with the new version's box placement and lettering, without asking Gemini again. (1.8.5 does this for every page: coloured lettering over pictures is now found whole and erased from the picture around it.) When a new version also asks Gemini differently (1.8.3: separate texts in one bubble or table as separate entries), **Rescan page** in the popup asks again for the page in front of you; **Clear saved translations** in settings makes every image be asked again.

## Speed and cost

| Model | Price per 1M tokens (in / out) | Approx. cost per 1,000 manga pages |
|---|---|---|
| `gemini-3.5-flash-lite` (default) | $0.30 / $2.50 | ~$1.20 |
| `gemini-3.1-flash-lite` (shuts down 7 May 2027) | $0.25 / $1.50 | ~$0.80 |
| `gemini-2.5-flash-lite` (only for keys that used it before 18 Sep 2026) | $0.10 / $0.40 | $0.20–0.30 |

Prices are from Google's pricing page as of 20 October 2026. On the Gemini 3 models a page is about 1,400 input tokens: a flat 1,120 per image plus the prompt. Output is about 150–300 tokens, and output is most of the bill. **Economy image detail** in settings halves the image tokens (560 on Gemini 3; on 2.5 it cuts them several times over) but can miss small text.

All three models are free on Google's free tier, within its limits. For the Flash-Lite models those are about 15 requests a minute and 500 a day. Google no longer publishes the exact figures; your project's limits are shown in AI Studio. The free-tier preset in settings matches them. Images on screen get a request each; images further down share requests, up to three to one, whenever the minute's allowance wouldn't last otherwise. When Google's "too many requests" reply names your project's per-minute limit, the extension paces itself at that limit until you change the model, key or plan. Google may use free-tier requests to improve its products.

Since 18 September 2026 Google serves the 2.5 models only to projects that used them before. Everyone else gets "no longer available to new users", which the extension reports with a one-click switch (see below).

**What makes it fast**
- Images on screen go first. Images up to three screens ahead (also inside scrollable reader panes), and up to two screens to either side (readers that turn pages by swiping), are translated before you reach them, so scrolling usually finds them done. Nothing further away is requested until you get close.
- **Reading one page at a time.** A reader that shows a page and loads the next one only when you turn to it has nothing ahead on the page to translate. So the extension watches the pages you're shown, and the next two are translated while you read, and show translated the moment you turn to them:
  - when the pictures' own addresses count: once two shown in a row count on by one (`…/012.jpg`, then `…/013.jpg`; `…?page=3`, then `…?page=4`);
  - when the reader's page addresses count (`…/read/12?page=3` after `?page=2`; `…/12/4` after `…/12/3`; or a code that changes as well, `…/s/8f3a/1234-4` after `…/s/c71e/1234-3`) — also two at a time, for readers showing two pages a view (`?page=1`, `?page=3`, `?page=5`): the next pages of the site are fetched in the background, as the browser would fetch them (with the site's own sign-in), only read, never run, and the picture in the place this page's is is translated;
  - when the reader loads the next pages in the background itself (to show them at once): those, where they're files in the same folder as the page on screen.

  A page only tells the worker which pictures it found; the worker decides, two after each page shown and a dozen a minute at most. Pages translated ahead never take the last free request: a page you've just turned to always gets one at once. **Translate ahead** set to 0 turns all of this off.
- Readers that lazy-load their pages keep each page's real address in an attribute (`data-src` and the like) until you scroll close. The extension translates from that address while the page is still screens away, and draws the result the moment the site shows it. It does so only for hosts the page already shows images from, so a comment can't point it elsewhere. It starts at most two pages ahead this way until the site has shown one of them as announced, and stops on that page if the site shows different files twice. A page that can't be downloaded this way (one that needs the site's login, say) is translated as usual once the site shows it.
- Images on screen go out first, each in a request of its own: up to four at a time on the free tier, more on paid plans. Images further down share requests when the minute's allowance is running low, or when pages come in faster than it can serve one by one (someone skimming a chapter). A lone one then waits up to 2.5 s for company. A shared answer arrives one image after another, so with allowance to spare each image goes alone.
- Gemini sometimes sits on a request for 15–20 s before answering. When no answer has started well past the usual wait, the same request is sent once more and whichever answers first is kept, the other cancelled. For an image on screen that's twice the recent typical time, 2.5–12 s (3.5 s until a few answers have been timed). For one further down it's 2.5 times, 4–12 s. Copies are sent only for images someone is still waiting for, count against **Requests at the same time**, and never go past your per-minute allowance or daily caps.
- The connection to Google is opened while the first image is still downloading, so the first request after a pause doesn't wait for it.
- Each image is decoded once. The pixels for placing translations, and the map of the lettering on the page, are prepared while Gemini works (one image at a time, between other work), so a bubble is drawn as soon as its part of the answer arrives.
- Lettering on artwork is first erased plainly, filled with the colours around it, which takes a fraction of the work, so its translation shows at once; on a phone that's seconds sooner. The texture of the artwork (screentone, hatching, grain) is carried in afterwards, one translation at a time and only while no page is waiting for its first translations, and each patch is redrawn in place without a flicker.
- While images are being translated, the popup says how many, and why they're waiting, if they are: your plan's per-minute limit, Google asking to slow down, or Gemini being slow.
- Responses are streamed, and each bubble is drawn the moment its JSON object arrives rather than when the whole answer is finished.
- Thinking is set to the minimum: off on 2.5, `MINIMAL` on 3.x, where it can't be switched off entirely. No temperature is sent to Gemini 3 models, as Google advises.
- Overlays are placed by the browser (CSS anchor positioning, Chrome 125+), so scrolling costs no work from the extension at all. A transform on the image itself (a centring shift, a zoom) is copied onto its overlay. Layout reads and writes are batched once per frame. On older Chrome, and for the rare image the browser won't anchor to (a sticky one, say), the overlay sits next to the image in the page's layout and is realigned in the same frame as each scroll.
- Images the page already decoded (same-origin, `blob:`, `data:`) are read from the page instead of being downloaded again.
- Cache lookups never wait behind slow downloads, and one busy site can't hold every work slot.
- Idle tabs don't keep a connection open, so the background worker can sleep. It wakes when there's something to translate.

**What keeps it cheap**
- Two caches in IndexedDB: by page + image URL (a reload doesn't even re-download) and by image content hash (the same image anywhere is translated once). Finished pages keep their erased patches, so that cache is held to about 150 MB, oldest pages going first.
- Identical requests from several tabs share one task. Jobs for images you scroll past before they start are cancelled.
- Images under the size threshold, SVGs and placeholders are skipped. The model is told to skip text that's already in your language.
- On the free tier, waiting images from the same site are packed several per request (a lone image is never held back).
- Hard daily caps on requests and estimated spend are enforced in the service worker, where a web page can't touch them. Each request reserves its worst-case cost (its whole output allowance) before it is sent, so parallel requests can't overshoot the cap. Near the cap, images wait for the requests in flight to settle rather than failing early. Requests that fail after Google has started answering still count, since they may be billed.
- A model that gets stuck repeating itself (a sound effect written out forever, the same bubble again and again, endless blank lines) is cut off as soon as the repetition shows, instead of being paid for up to the token limit. Once the JSON answer is complete, the extension stops listening even if the model keeps padding.
- An image whose answer got stuck in a loop, or brought nothing usable, gets exactly one more try: alone, sampled a little more freely. An answer that merely ran out of room isn't paid for twice. Whatever came through is kept and cached, marked as incomplete. If one image makes Gemini refuse a shared request, the others are asked again on their own rather than failing with it.
- A request field the API rejects by name (the response schema, say) is left off afterwards, for that model only. An unexplained rejection only affects the request at hand, so it can never switch off the settings that keep answers cheap.

## Where the text goes, and how big

Vision models place boxes roughly: shifted by half a bubble or more, around one column of a vertical block, around the furigana on their own, sometimes all of them a little way off in the same direction. So the boxes only say which translation goes with which text; where the text is comes from the image.

- **The text map** (`background/textmap.js`) finds every block of lettering on the image, before Gemini's answer arrives. It marks pixels clearly darker (or lighter) than their surroundings, joins them into pieces, the pieces into characters (the strokes of a kanji, the letters of a Hangul syllable, a dakuten on its kana, the parallel strokes of 三), the characters into straight columns and lines of one size, and those into blocks with their furigana, punctuation and dashes. A block must stand in a margin of paper (a bubble, a caption box, an outline around the letters) and look like text, which is what tells it apart from screentone, hatching, speed lines, hair and other drawing; a "character" surrounded by many pieces just like it (screentone dots clumped together, a band of hatching) is texture. Every page is looked at the same size: a larger scan is averaged down to it first, so its screentone, linework and lettering look as they would on any other page, and erasing measures its outlines and textures to match. Lettering in a colour edged in white (or black), set straight over dark, busy art — a game CG's pink lines over foliage — is found by its edging too: coloured strokes with much lighter (or darker) pixels close on both sides; the letters and their edging, seen once as dark on light and once as light on dark, are one block. In a colour picture, lettering in a colour of its own (pink, purple, orange…) ringed by an outline is also found by exactly that (`background/colortext.js`): pieces of strong colour edged nearly all round in a light (or dark) neutral tone, joined into columns and lines of one size and colour — with the "…" and small kana in them, set either way — and those side by side into texts, whose map blocks make way for them; over a bright, busy picture the brightness alone may see such a column in pieces, or joined to the drawing beside it. A patch of the picture closed in by edgings (sky between two columns), a leaf on its shadow or a strip of sky between wires is not taken: nothing much smaller than the picture's dialogue counts, and a word or two alone must be edged in white, as a sound effect is. It takes about 0.05–0.2 s per page (cut off at 1.5 s; anything not found by then falls back to the search below) and no API call.
- **Matching** (`background/match.js`): a translation whose box clearly lies on one block takes it at once, so answers still stream onto the page. The rest wait until Gemini has finished with the image and are then matched together, by distance, size, shape and the length of the translation, so two boxes that both missed can't swap their text. A box fitting text much better than the box that took it takes it over. A text Gemini gives in two parts (its columns or lines apart) gets one translation, made of both parts in order, over all of it; the same text described twice (its furigana on their own) is drawn once — and so is a text given twice with the second box on other lettering (a caption given again over the sound effect beside it): it isn't run on into that one's translation, nor drawn over lettering far too small for it. If most boxes are off the same way, by the same shift or as if the image were scaled, the error is measured from them and taken out before matching — from the blocks they should lie on, or, where few blocks were found whole, from the lettering's ink: the shift or scale that puts the most of it inside the boxes, provided it suits most boxes on their own too. A box with no lettering under it moves to text at most two box-lengths away, never across to another panel's. A translation Gemini gives again in part — one entry for a whole text, another for its first column ("…, you know" beside "…, it's just that I won't beat her") — is drawn once: when one's words are near all in the other's, in their order, and their lettering lies side by side, the part's lettering goes with the whole translation.
- **Texts set apart stay apart; one text stays together** (`background/texts.js`, `background/bubbles.js`). Lettering with clear space between it is separate texts, each translated on its own over its own lettering, in one bubble or box too: two remarks in one bubble ("えーと…" set apart from the question after it), a caption's two paragraphs, the fields of a game's status screen or a table (a label and its value, each row's fields), a sign's lines, a map's labels. Clear space is most of a character across, and well over the spacing of the columns (or lines) around it, or a character and a half however the rest is spaced; along a line, most of a character. A table's fields stand alone however close its rows are set, a value Gemini skips (a number on its own) stays as it is, and a field Gemini boxes only in part at a space ("スタミナ" of "スタミナ20": the label, not its value) is cut there. Lettering of one text that the map made several blocks of (a column set apart, its furigana, characters that made no block like a lone "私……") goes with the translation over any of it, and two translations over one text become one, in the order Gemini gave them. For that, the bubble around each block is found: from the paper among its letters, a flood fill that the bubble's outline closes in (a caption box of any shape too; a gap of a few pixels in the outline doesn't let it out). When Gemini gives one translation for a whole bubble of two remarks, the other remark goes with it too if its box takes in a good part of it, or if the translation is far longer than the lettering its box took. A drawing inside a bubble — a little figure under the text, a heart — is no part of its lettering: one or two "characters" far larger than the rest of it are left alone. Text on a panel's open background is no bubble, and two bubbles that touch stay two.
- **Lettering the map made no block of** (`background/guided.js`): text over busy art, or set right beside drawing the map took for part of it (fingers under a line of narration, lace, a window frame), may make no block though each of its characters was found. Where Gemini's box lies on such characters, they become the block — characters of one size close together, columns followed on past the box's edge, the dots of a "……" to the last — and it is erased as exactly as any other. A block found only in part (some of a narration's columns) gets the rest of its characters the same way: those in line with its own (its next column, its column running on), not drawing that merely lies inside its box. Over busy art the map may see letters edged in white as their edging only, or merge a letter with the drawing beside it (or with the next column's letter): the edging, down the column, counts as the letters, and a merged character is cut back to the column it is in (not a shape the map made a block of on its own: that is a drawing it took for lettering, not a letter run into one). A box with only a little of its lettering in blocks takes those and the characters around them in the box, rather than some block further off.
- Text the map didn't find falls back to a search for lettering around Gemini's box (`background/refine.js`), as before. When neither finds lettering, the box is used as it is.
- The size of the original characters is measured too. A translation is set at most 10% larger than that, so a short "No way" in a roomy bubble stays the size of the dialogue around it — and at most 1.5× the size of the image's dialogue (the size most of its characters are set at), so a huge sound effect's translation stays a caption beside the drawing rather than a banner across it. Long text shrinks to fit with its words kept whole; a word is broken only if even the smallest size can't fit it.
- The text colour is sampled from the cores of the original strokes, so black lettering stays black rather than turning grey from its anti-aliased edges.
- In a bubble, the translation gets the part of it where it can be set largest. In a tall, narrow bubble made for vertical Japanese, that's usually a wide band across its middle, not the tallest column. On artwork, the translation is set over its own erased lettering — the clean ground the erasing leaves, where nothing of the drawing competes with it — and widened past it only as far as its text needs to be set at about two thirds of the original's character size (a single column of vertical lettering by up to three characters, evenly about its middle). It takes the original lettering's colours — pink letters edged in white stay pink edged in white — with a solid outline round every letter, so it reads over busy art. A short word its translation outgrows (a field of a status screen) gets the plain paper around it as well, across first and then up and down evenly, keeping clear of the next lettering (its value stays readable beside it).
- Translations never share space. Where two would, the space is split between their lettering, along the side where the two lie apart, and each keeps its own side; one already on screen is set again in its share.
- Lettering with nothing to translate, like "!?" or "…", is left as it is.
- Translations are set in capitals, as comics are lettered (**Capital letters** in settings; off: ordinary upper and lower case). Language-aware: the page's target language decides how letters are capitalised.

Placing has a time budget per image and per minute. When it runs out, the rest of that image keeps the model's boxes, and the result isn't saved, so the next visit places them properly from Gemini's saved answer without asking it again.

## How the original lettering is hidden

- **In a clean bubble** (the area around the text is all the bubble's colour), the text is painted over in that colour (`background/analysis.js` finds it, and the bubble, with a colour histogram and a flood fill — of the paper's tone as the text map measured it, so a box tight round a word in a heavy face, more ink than paper, is still painted white). A bubble the artwork shows through (a game's translucent ones) is not clean: its text is erased, so the artwork stays.
- **Everywhere else** — on artwork, or where painting over would cover more than plain paper (hatching between the letters' outlines, a bubble's edge) — the lettering is erased (`background/inpaint.js`). Exactly its glyphs are taken, from the text map, and their outline: the band of another tone right around them (a white outline round black letters, a black one round white), measured from where it meets the artwork, so the erasing stops where the artwork begins. Whatever the letters close in goes with them: their counters, and letters found only as their edging (the colour inside the white). The patch is an image made in the worker and drawn under the translation.
- **Lettering on plain paper** — a bubble whose text comes close to its outline, a caption box of a shape no rectangle fits, a page's margin beside the art, a black box with white letters — is that paper again once erased, and nothing else. The paper is recognised right around the glyphs: mostly one flat tone (give or take its noise), reaching on past their own surroundings (an edging round each letter, with artwork beyond it, is not paper), with no texture on it. Each erased pixel then takes the paper's colour from the paper's own pixels only, so the bubble's outline, the next bubble's text or the drawing beyond a caption's edge can't be blended in as a grey smudge where the letters were. Where a letter ran into a shape (a lock of hair, the outline), the shape is carried on a few pixels over what was ink, not grown across the erased letters. The faint grey a JPEG leaves around letters goes with them, so no ghost of the text stays behind, and so do the lettering's small marks the map took for no character: ruby (furigana), the dots of a "……", a long "——" or "〜〜" down a column — pieces on the paper by the glyphs no larger than a mark, or as thin as one and a few characters long along the text.
- **Coloured lettering on artwork** — a game CG's pink letters edged in white over trees and sky — is completed by its colour before it is erased (`background/complete.js`): over busy art the map may find a column in pieces, merge its first character with the drawing beside it, or take a "（笑）" at its end for part of the picture. The rest of it is the pieces of the letters' colour along the translation's own columns (or lines), within them and on past either end while they keep coming, each no larger than a character and ringed by the lettering's edging — never another translation's lettering, nor a thing of the same colour in the drawing (no edging), nor a text off to the side. So no half-character or last word is left beside the translation. (Lettering found by its colour in the first place, above, is whole already.) (Black or white lettering is left as the map found it: its colour is the drawing's own.)
- An outline band round the letters with the artwork right past it is never taken for paper, however plain the band and whatever light area of the drawing it touches here and there: what comes back is the artwork, not the outline's white. The band is measured from the letters' anti-aliased edge, so all of it goes.
- **Lettering on artwork** is filled from its surroundings: thin lines and dots around it count for little, so a colour patch comes back clean, not smeared. Where the surroundings are textured — screentone, hatching, grain — each letter-shaped piece is instead replaced by a copy of the artwork just beside it whose surroundings match its own best, brought to its colours, so the texture carries on through where the letters were. Copies are never taken from other lettering, nor from artwork of another colour (grey wall for blue sky): in colour art a copy's surroundings must match in colour, not just brightness, or the smooth fill stays.
- **In a colour picture** (a game CG, a webtoon) the erased lettering over the picture is first filled across from both sides — a wire, a desk's edge or a window frame behind a column carried straight on through — and then, when the texture is carried in, from small squares of the picture around it (`background/patchfill.js`: PatchMatch inpainting, coarse to fine). Every square over the erased area is matched with the most alike square nearby — never from the erased pixels, the glow round them, or other lettering — and each pixel takes the colour most of the squares over it agree on: foliage carries on as foliage and a wall as a wall, not as a blur or streaks, and letter-shaped copies of other parts of the picture don't appear.
- A large patch of erased lettering (a whole multi-column narration whose outlines run together) is healed tile by tile from its edge inwards, each tile continuing the artwork right beside it and brought to its brightness by their typical difference, so a few lines that don't line up can't grey a whole tile. Where the artwork around it is line art running one way (pillars, curtains, speed lines), each line is carried straight on through it instead, taken from where the line runs on clear of the glow the lettering left on it, so no pale silhouette of the column is left. A soft glow around the letters is taken with them, out to where the artwork's lines resume.
- Erasing has its own time budget per image. On a page so full of lettering that it runs out, the rest is still erased exactly, filled with its surroundings' plain colours rather than their texture, and the page is done properly on the next visit from Gemini's saved answer.
- Only when no lettering at all could be found under a box is the area washed out, lightly and tightly around Gemini's box.

**Speech-bubble detection** in settings moves the threshold between bubbles and artwork.

## Security model

The service worker is the only trusted party; pages and content scripts are treated as hostile clients.

- **API key:** stored in the extension's own IndexedDB, which web pages and content scripts can't open. It's sent only to Google's endpoint, which is a constant in the code, not a setting, so nothing stored anywhere can redirect it.
- **Settings and usage:** also in the extension's IndexedDB. Content scripts can't read or change caps or sites on any Chrome version, and the extension doesn't request the `storage` permission at all. The end-to-end test checks this from inside the content script's own world.
- **Messages:** every port message is schema-checked and rate-limited, and the sender's identity comes from Chrome (`port.sender`), never from the message. A page reporting images it loaded ahead (its reader's next pages) only gets them translated if the worker finds them beside a page it showed lately, or (pictures of the next pages of a reader with an address per page) after a page it showed lately; two after each page shown, a dozen a minute at most. Those next pages are fetched by the content script from the page's own site only, read with `DOMParser` (no script of theirs runs, no image of theirs loads). A site whose scripts keep misbehaving is locked out for 10 minutes; reconnecting doesn't reset that. Job limits are per tab. Spending limits live only in the worker.
- **Batching:** only images from the same site share a model request. Text inside one site's image therefore can't steer the translation of another site's image. Answers from shared requests are cached for that site only.
- **Fetching:** URLs must be `http(s)`, with no credentials in the URL. Requests are sent without cookies, so a hostile page can't use the extension to read images only you can see on another site. Private and loopback addresses are refused unless the page itself is on that host. A network rule enforces this for the worker's own requests, so a redirect into your local network is stopped before it's sent. Size caps are enforced while streaming. Hotlink-protected hosts get the page's `Referer`, through a rule that applies only to this extension's own requests.
- **Image content:** finding the lettering, placing translations and erasing are pixel work in the worker, with fixed limits: the text map works at no more than 1.7 megapixels, stops at a cap on pieces of ink and after 1.5 s, erasing is limited in area and time, placing has a time budget per image and per minute, and the worker takes a turn for other work every 40 ms. Matching hundreds of boxes stays fast (a greedy match beyond what the exact one does in tens of milliseconds). An image built to be expensive to analyse only loses its own fine placement; it can't hold up the extension.
- **Rendering:** model output is inserted with `textContent` into a closed shadow root. There is no `innerHTML` and no `eval`, and the extension pages use the default MV3 CSP. Erased patches are made in the worker and reach the page as bytes (size-capped, checked to be base64 WebP or PNG); they're decoded with `createImageBitmap` and drawn onto a canvas, so no URL is ever loaded for them and a site's content security policy plays no part.
- **Access to websites:** granted at install, since the images a site shows come from servers of its own choosing; asking later depends on a prompt some browsers (on phones) can't show. It's used only for the sites you switch on: content scripts are registered for those sites alone, and only their scripts can ask the worker for an image.
- **Footprint:** content scripts run only on sites you switch on. There are no `web_accessible_resources`, so sites can't detect the extension, and none of its globals are visible to the page.
- **Known residual risk:** a public domain name that resolves to a private IP address can't be told apart from an extension. Because no cookies are sent, only unauthenticated images on your local network would be exposed, and only on sites you've switched on.

## When something goes wrong

The popup says what happened, with a **Details** line underneath carrying Google's own error text (or the extension's own reason, such as "the model got stuck repeating itself"). The same details go to the worker's console: `chrome://extensions` → Image Translator → **service worker** → Console, one line per distinct error per minute, starting with `[imgtr]`.

If Google doesn't offer the selected model to your key, the popup names the model and offers **Switch to Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite**. One click picks it, and open pages translate again; images finished earlier come back from the cache. **Save key** and **Test key** in settings catch the same problem up front, as does changing the model there.

Problems with the key, your limits or the connection clear themselves as soon as Gemini answers again. Problems with particular images stay listed until you click **Rescan page** or reload. A reload repaints finished and incomplete images from the cache for free and asks again only for images that failed. **Rescan page** also asks again for incomplete ones, and for ones answered before the current version of the prompt. Only the popup can trigger that, so a web page can't make the extension pay twice for an answer it already has.

## Limitations

- Only `<img>` elements are translated. CSS background images, readers that draw pages onto `<canvas>`, and images inside web components (shadow DOM) are not.
- While an overlay is anchored, its image carries an extra CSS anchor name in its `style` attribute. Any anchor names the page gave the image are kept, and the original attribute is put back when the overlay goes. A page that copies the image (zoom views, lightboxes) gets the copy cleaned up, and one that rewrites the attribute has the name put back before anything is drawn.
- Images on other sites that need your login cookies can't be downloaded; this is deliberate (see the security model). Same-origin images work because they're read from the page.
- Animated GIFs use their first frame.
- Erasing rebuilds what was behind the letters from what's around them. Flat colour, gradients and textures that repeat (screentone, hatching, grain) come back cleanly; detailed drawing behind large letters (a face, a hand, a row of desks behind a column of a CG's lettering) comes back as a soft, sometimes patchy continuation of its surroundings.
- An image in something you're editing (a mail being written, a document) isn't translated, so no overlay ends up in what you write.
- Quality is the model's. 3.5 Flash-Lite is the newest Flash-Lite and reads stylised lettering and sound effects best. 3.1 Flash-Lite is about 30% cheaper until it shuts down.
- The bundled lettering font (Comic Neue) covers Latin scripts; other scripts fall back to system fonts. Japanese and Traditional Chinese targets switch to vertical writing in tall boxes.
- The size limit for translations is measured from the original characters. For Japanese, Chinese and Korean it matches what you see. For Latin-script originals it measures capital or lowercase height, so translations into another Latin-script language may come out somewhat smaller than the original.

## Development

```bash
npm install          # TypeScript + Chrome types, for checking only — the extension has no build step
npm run typecheck    # strict type-check of all JS via JSDoc
npm test             # unit tests: lettering found, matched and erased on realistic manga pages, box placement, JSON streaming, analysis, limits, settings, Gemini client
npm run test:e2e     # needs Python 3 + Pillow + a CJK font, and Playwright with Chromium
E2E_NO_ANCHORS=1 npm run test:e2e   # the same, as on Chrome < 125 (overlays placed without anchor positioning)
```

The unit tests for lettering run on five generated manga pages with per-pixel truth (`tests/unit/fixtures/manga.py`; Pillow and Noto CJK to regenerate): dialogue in bubbles with furigana, narration in open space and straight on screentone, outlined text over hatching, hair and leaded glass, white-on-black captions, rotated, hollow and outlined sound effects over screentone, speed lines and busy colour art, Korean and Chinese in balloons and captions, English in a balloon, thin and tiny type. They check that every block is found whole and nothing else is — also with the pages resampled to 0.7×, 1.3×, 1.6× and 2× their size — that translations land on their own text under every kind of box error (shifted, too small or large, one column, all off the same way, scaled, a bubble given in two parts), that lettering the map made no block of is still taken whole from its characters where the box is, and that erasing takes all of the lettering and visibly changes nothing else, with screentone carried on through. A white bubble built for the purpose — a column of letters right beside a dark shape, with JPEG-like ringing round them, ruby beside them and a long dash down the column — must come out white where all of that was (at most a few levels off the paper), with the dark shape untouched. On those pages, sound effects' translations must come out no larger than 1.5× the dialogue. A generated game CG (`manga.py cg`: pink lettering edged in white over sky, a house and foliage) checks that such lettering is erased whole under rough boxes, each translation in its own space; hand-built text maps check the pieces (`tests/unit/guided.test.mjs`). Synthetic colour pictures check that lettering in a colour of its own is found by its colour and edging as one block (not a flower of its colour, nor a text off to the side), that a wire behind an erased column is carried straight on through, and that filling from squares of the picture keeps its colours and texture and never copies other lettering (`tests/unit/colour.test.mjs`). A generated page of texts set apart (`manga.py texts`: a game's status window with a table of fields and a label run into its value, a caption in two paragraphs, a remark set apart in a bubble, two lines of one text, a sound effect beside the caption) checks that each field, paragraph and remark is a text of its own, that translations given field by field, row by row or all in one each cover exactly their own lettering and leave the values Gemini skipped as they are, and that a caption Gemini gives twice, once over the sound effect, is drawn once (`tests/unit/texts.test.mjs`).

The end-to-end test loads the extension into headless Chromium and serves a manga page, a tall webtoon strip in a nested scroller, same-origin, `blob:`, hotlink-protected, lazy-loaded and late-inserted images. It points the extension at a mock Gemini server that places its boxes roughly, the way Gemini does, and checks the translations, that bubbles are painted over and the sound effect on artwork is erased exactly, that the patches hide all of the original lettering, that translations are set no larger than it, that a lazy-loading page is translated ahead from the address the site keeps for it, overlay alignment, caching on reload, popup controls (the text size re-setting an open page's translations at once, with no new requests) and the storage isolation. An image inside an editable area gets no overlay, and one made editable later loses its own. Three readers show one page at a time: one whose picture addresses count, one that loads the next page in the background under a name that doesn't count, and one with an address per page (`book.html?page=N`, a whole new page to turn to the next); in all three, the next page is translated before it's turned to and shows translated within a few frames of it, with no request for it then. A scroll-layouts page puts images where overlays are hardest to keep on them: in a reader whose `<body>` is the scroller, bordered and padded in a centred flex column, moved by script, scaled by a transform, fixed in a corner, sticky, copied by the page, with their `style` attribute rewritten, and one the browser won't anchor to. It checks, within the first frame after each scroll, that every overlay is exactly on its image. A second page covers failures. One answer gets stuck in a loop: it is cut off, retried once and then complete. One stays stuck on both tries: the bubble that came through is kept, a reload is free, and **Rescan** asks again. Gemini never answers one: it is retried once, then reported in the popup with its details. Finally the model is switched to one the mock refuses with Google's own "no longer available to new users" reply. The checks: the key test says so, the popup names the model and quotes Google, only the first refusals are sent, and one click switches back with everything repainting from the cache. The worker's console is watched throughout, and any unexpected error or warning fails the run.

`npm run build:font` regenerates `content/font.js` from `fonts/*.woff2`, and `npm run build:icons` redraws the icons.
